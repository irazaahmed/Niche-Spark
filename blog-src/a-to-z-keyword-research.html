The A to Z method is the simplest systematic way to explore a topic with Google Autocomplete. You type your seed keyword followed by each letter of the alphabet, one at a time, and record what Google predicts. Twenty-six queries later you have a map of how people actually search around your topic.

Why adding a letter works

When you type just your seed, Google shows a small number of predictions, usually the strongest ones. Everything else stays hidden. Adding a letter after a space tells Google the next word starts with that letter, so it has to show different completions:

  • mobile car detailing e → equipment, el paso, elk grove
  • mobile car detailing i → in my area, insurance, interior, income
  • mobile car detailing q → queens, quincy, quote

Each letter is a new window into the long tail. These examples are real predictions from our mobile car detailing case study, where 26 A to Z queries alone produced 252 keywords no other query found.

How to run an A to Z pass

  1. Choose your seed. Use the phrase your audience would type, in the language they use. Two to four words works best.
  2. Pick the right Google. Use the country domain and language of your market. Predictions are localized.
  3. Type the seed, a space and a letter. The space matters: detailing a asks for a next word starting with a; detailinga does not.
  4. Wait for the dropdown to finish. Predictions arrive in a moment and can update as they load. Read the final list, not the first flash.
  5. Record every prediction with its letter. You'll want to know which query produced which keyword later.
  6. Repeat for b to z, then merge duplicates.

By hand, this is 26 rounds of typing, waiting and copying. NicheSpark does the same loop automatically in a background tab: it types each query, waits for the dropdown to settle, saves the predictions with the letter that produced them, and skips your own search-history entries. You can also start from any letter if you want to continue a previous run.

Be patient with pacing. Run queries one at a time with a pause between them, as a person would. If Google ever shows a verification page, stop and complete it yourself.

How to read the results

Raw A to Z output looks chaotic at first. Read it for themes, not individual keywords. In a typical run you'll see a few recurring kinds of completion:

Placesmobile car detailing raleigh ncLocal intent; candidate location pages
Price wordsmobile car detailing prices listBuyers comparing costs
Sub-servicesmobile car detailing interiorSpecific service pages
Business intentmobile car detailing business for saleA second audience: owners and starters
Brand namesxtreme mobile car detailingCompetitors, not targets

Once you've spotted the themes, you know which prefixes and suffixes to research next. If "cost" keeps appearing, run cost, prices and quote as suffixes to dig deeper.

Common mistakes

Treating every prediction as a target

A to Z returns noise: distant cities, other countries, business names. Filter for relevance before planning content. A detailing business in North Carolina doesn't need a page for mobile car detailing zurich.

Ignoring the hard letters

Letters like q, u, x, y and z often look empty or odd, but they can surface useful outliers. In our case study, q gave us quote and m gave us mold removal near me, a service you might not have thought to research.

Expecting Google to complete every letter the same way

For letters with no natural next word, Google may show phrases where the letter starts a word before the seed. Every x suggestion in our data was a business name like xlr8 mobile car detailing. That's normal behavior, not an error.

Forgetting that results vary

Location, language, trends and your own sign-in state influence predictions. Your list is a snapshot of what Google showed you at that moment, so date your research and rerun it occasionally.

Mistaking presence for popularity

A to Z tells you a phrase is searched, not how often. Google itself notes that autocomplete doesn't simply show the most common queries. Check volume in a metrics tool before betting a big project on one keyword.

Going further: two-level A to Z

Once you've found a strong theme, run A to Z again on a longer seed. For example, after discovering mobile car detailing prices, research mobile car detailing prices a to z. This digs a level deeper into one corner of the niche. Use it selectively: two-level research multiplies the number of queries quickly, so keep the pace slow and the scope focused.

Frequently asked questions

How long does an A to Z pass take?

Twenty-six queries with a few seconds between each takes a few minutes. Doing it manually, including copying, usually takes much longer.

Should I include numbers as well as letters?

For most niches letters are enough. Numbers can help in topics where years, sizes or model numbers matter, like software versions or product sizes.

Is A to Z enough on its own?

It's the best starting point, but in our case study prefixes and suffixes still found 56 keywords A to Z missed. Use A to Z for breadth, then modifiers for depth.

What's next after I have the list?

Group keywords by topic and intent, give each group one page, and avoid targeting the same group twice. The complete autocomplete guide covers this step in detail.
